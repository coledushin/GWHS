# U.S. History & Government Regents Exam
## Question-Trend Analysis and June 2026 Content Forecast

*Prepared May 2026 — Based on nine administrations, June 2023 – January 2026*

---

## Executive Summary

This report analyzes nine consecutive administrations of the New York State Regents Examination in U.S. History and Government (June 2023 through January 2026) to identify how exam content is distributed and how it shifts from test to test. The goal is to forecast the most probable content of the June 2026 examination at both the framework-unit level and the more granular content-standard level.

The central finding is that Regents content is **not random**. It follows identifiable patterns: a stable structural framework, a small set of "iron-man" sub-topics that recur almost every exam, and a rotation logic in which heavily used content rests and dormant content returns. *Predictions built on era-level frequency alone are insufficient — the data show that topics trend in and out, and that recent saturation in one part of the exam (for example, the essays) suppresses the same content elsewhere.*

## 1. Data and Methodology

The analysis draws on a question-by-question itemization of every released Regents exam from June 2023 through January 2026. Each multiple-choice question was coded to its NYS framework unit (11.01–11.11) and, where possible, to its specific leaf-level content standard (e.g., 11.09a.2, Containment Policies). Each question was further tagged for stimulus type (text, visual, map, data, multiple) and for whether it requires outside information ("OI") beyond the document itself.

**Note on the two most recent exams:** the source itemization workbook covers administrations through June 2025. The August 2025 and January 2026 exams were coded directly from the released PDFs for this report. Those two columns are therefore the analyst's readings rather than the workbook author's official coding; they are directionally reliable but should be treated as estimates.

All counts below refer to Part I multiple-choice questions unless the written portions (Part II short-essay questions and the Part III Civic Literacy Essay) are explicitly named.

## 2. How Regents Questions Behave: Six Structural Trends

Before forecasting specific content, it is essential to understand the recurring mechanics that govern how the exam is built. Six patterns emerge consistently across all nine administrations.

### Trend 1 — The format is fixed; the content rotates
Every exam follows the same architecture: 28 stimulus-based multiple-choice questions, two short-essay questions (each built on a pair of documents), and one Civic Literacy document-based essay. Every single question is anchored to a source — there are no "naked" recall questions. This stability means the variable to predict is not format but content placement.

### Trend 2 — Roughly two-thirds of questions require outside knowledge
Across the dataset, about **67%** of multiple-choice questions cannot be answered from the document alone; they require the student to supply outside information. Critically, this burden is unevenly distributed across both content and stimulus type, which has direct teaching implications (see Section 4).

### Trend 3 — Within each unit, questions cluster on a few "iron-man" standards
A framework unit is not tested evenly across its sub-topics. Cold War questions overwhelmingly mean *Containment*; the Depression era overwhelmingly means the *New Deal*. Predicting at the era level alone misses this concentration — the unit label is a container for a handful of repeatedly favored standards (see Section 5).

### Trend 4 — The written and multiple-choice portions are complementary
When a topic anchors an essay, it tends to be rested in the multiple-choice section, and vice versa. This is the single most important refinement to era-level forecasting. The Prosperity-and-Depression unit (11.07) is the clearest example: its multiple-choice presence has collapsed to almost nothing precisely as it has dominated the essays.

### Trend 5 — Topics rotate; rebounds are about twice as common as sustained declines
When a unit's question count drops from one exam to the next, the most likely next move is a rebound. Across all dips in the dataset, the following exam **rebounded 48% of the time, stayed flat 30%, and fell further only 22%**. Equivalently, a low reading is more often a trough than the start of a trend. The corollary also holds: two unusually high exams in a row typically precede a rest.

### Trend 6 — Constitutional Foundations is the backbone
Across nine exams, 11.02 (Constitutional Foundations) averages nearly five questions — the highest of any unit — and has anchored both multiple-choice sets and the short-essay section. It is the most reliable single unit on the exam, which makes its recent two-exam decline (Section 3) the most consequential anomaly in the current data.

### Reading the unit table
- **11.02, 11.06, 11.05, and 11.03** are the high-volume backbone of the exam, each averaging well over four questions.

- **The standout current anomaly is 11.02's decline** from a steady 5–6 to 4 (Aug 2025) and then 2 (Jan 2026) — the first back-to-back decline this unit has ever recorded.

### Do dips rebound or persist? The rotation evidence
To test whether low readings tend to recover, every exam-over-exam decline in the dataset was classified by what happened on the very next administration.

| Outcome after a dip | Frequency | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Rebound (next exam higher) | 13 | 48% |
| Flat (next exam equal) | 8 | 30% |
| Deeper (next exam lower) | 6 | 22% |

**Unit-specific behavior matters as much as the aggregate:**

- **Reliable rebounders:** 11.05, 11.08, 11.09, and 11.06 have never sustained a multi-exam decline without bouncing back — 11.09 and 11.05 already rebounded in January 2026.
- **The exception — 11.07:** Prosperity & Depression is the one unit that has never rebounded. After peaking at 8 (Jan 2024) it has fallen or held flat for five straight exams. This is not a dip awaiting recovery; it is a sustained withdrawal from multiple choice (explained by Trend 4).
- **The watch item — 11.02:** its only prior dip (Jan 2024) rebounded immediately. The current double-dip is unprecedented, so the base rate favors a strong rebound — but it is the prediction carrying the most genuine uncertainty.

Stimulus type tells a parallel story. Visual-based questions require outside knowledge 93% of the time (the image carries little factual content on its own), while questions with multiple short stimuli are answerable from the documents 90% of the time.

**Teaching implication:** the two most-tested hard units (11.02 and 11.05) combine high volume with high outside-information demand, so they reward content mastery, not test-taking tricks. Conversely, Reconstruction (11.04) and World War II (11.08) — especially Japanese Internment — are the best point-per-effort targets because students can frequently reason directly from the document.

### Applying rotation logic at the standard level

**Due to return (high lifetime frequency, currently cold):**
- **11.03a.1 American Nationalism** — a former staple (8 lifetime) with zero appearances across the last three exams. The most overdue high-frequency standard on the test.
- **11.09a.2 Containment** — the single most-tested standard overall; it has never skipped two exams in a row and was absent in January 2026. A near-lock to return.

**Recently saturated, likely to rest:**
- **11.06a.2 Spanish-American War** — tested in both of the last two exams; two-in-a-row reliably precedes a pause.
- **11.05b.1 Urbanization & Immigration** — hot in four of the last five exams; due to cool.

### Blind spots: high-profile standards never tested in multiple choice

If the examiners reach for fresh content, they will draw from canonical standards that have **never appeared as a multiple-choice topic** in this nine-exam window. These are defensive review items — students assume they are fair game because they are famous, yet the exam has avoided them:

- **Cold War:** Cuban Missile Crisis (11.09b.2), Détente (11.09b.3), Camp David Accords (11.09c.1)
- **World War II:** Pearl Harbor (11.08a.3), the Holocaust (11.08c.1), the Atomic Bomb (11.08a.5)
- **Early Republic / Civil War:** Hamilton's Economic Plan (11.02d.2), Union vs. Confederacy (11.03c.1), Gettysburg Address (11.03c.3)
- **1920s:** Roaring Twenties (11.07a.1), Hoover vs. FDR (11.07c.3)

## 6. The Written Portions and the "Rest" Effect

The complementarity between the essays and multiple choice (Trend 4) is the strongest argument against naive era-level forecasting. Below is the content of the written portions on the two most recent exams.

| Component | August 2025 | January 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Short-Essay 1 | Declaration + Constitution (11.02) | Great Depression charts + FDR (11.07) |
| Short-Essay 2 | FDR "Forgotten Man" letter (11.07) | Erie Canal / Market Revolution (11.03) |
| Civic Literacy Essay | Government Regulation of Business (11.05) | Prohibition (11.07) |

**11.07 occupied three of the six written slots across these two exams.** The correct inference is *not* that the Depression era is "building toward" a multiple-choice comeback — it is that the exam has been satisfying its 11.07 coverage obligation through the essays, which is precisely why its multiple-choice count has gone to near zero. By the same rotation logic, recently-used essay topics (Prohibition, the Erie Canal, Government Regulation of Business) are unlikely to anchor the June 2026 essays.

### Most probable June 2026 essay anchors
- **Civic Literacy Essay:** Civil Rights Movement (11.10) is the leading candidate — a perennial civic-issue topic not used recently. Women's Suffrage and Immigration are secondary candidates.
- **Short-Essay sets:** fresh material from the Cold War (11.09) and the WWI era (11.06) is most likely, as neither has anchored a recent written prompt.

## 7. June 2026 Forecast and the Reasoning Behind It

The predictions in the Executive Summary follow directly from the trends above. The reasoning for each high-confidence call:

### 11.02 Constitutional Foundations — forecast 5–6, High
The highest-volume unit on the exam (4.9 average) is at an all-time low after an unprecedented double-dip. The base-rate rebound logic (Trend 5), the unit's status as the backbone (Trend 6), and the fact that it last anchored an essay in August 2025 (freeing it to return to multiple choice under Trend 4) all point the same direction.

### 11.09 Cold War — forecast 4–5, Very High
The unit rebounded to 4 in January 2026 and is a reliable rebounder. More decisively, its iron-man standard — Containment (11.09a.2) — was absent in January 2026 and has never skipped two exams running, making its return the most predictable single event on the test. The unit is also fresh for the essays.

### 11.03 Expansion & Sectionalism — forecast 3–5, High
After spiking to 8 in June 2025, the unit fell to 2 for two exams — below its 4.4 average and primed for recovery. Within it, American Nationalism (11.03a.1) is the most overdue staple on the entire exam. The unit's recent essay appearance (Erie Canal, Jan 2026) means that specific content is unlikely to repeat, but the multiple-choice pressure should flow back.

### 11.07 Prosperity & Depression — forecast 1–2, Low (the key contrarian call)
This is where era-level intuition fails most clearly. A naive model sees a unit at a low and predicts a rebound; a naive model also sees 11.07 dominating recent essays and assumes that signals importance. **Both are wrong. 11.07 is the one unit that has never rebounded, and its essay dominance is the cause of — not a precursor to — its multiple-choice disappearance.** Having filled three of six recent essay slots, it is more likely to rest across the entire exam than to surge anywhere. It should be taught for knowledge, not weighted as a probable high-yield topic.

### Lower-confidence units
- **11.06 (4–5, High):** steady performer, fresh for essays — but expect a topic *other than* the Spanish-American War, which has now appeared twice running.
- **11.05 (3–4, Medium):** anchored the August 2025 Civic Literacy Essay, so a multiple-choice surge is unlikely; expect a moderate, steady presence.
- **11.10 (3–5, Medium):** if it becomes the June 2026 Civic Literacy topic (likely), its multiple-choice count may stay moderate rather than spike, as the exam tends not to double up.
- **11.04, 11.08, 11.01, 11.11 (Low):** structurally light units; useful for breadth and, in the case of 11.04 and 11.08, for high-efficiency document-based points.

## 8. Limitations

- Nine administrations is a modest sample; single-exam swings can be large, and confidence levels reflect tendencies, not guarantees.
- The August 2025 and January 2026 codings are the analyst's readings of released PDFs, not the official itemization, and may differ at the margins from the test author's intended coding.
- Rotation logic is probabilistic. The exam writers are not bound by it, and an unusually high or low reading can persist contrary to the base rate — the 11.02 forecast in particular carries real uncertainty.
- Forecasts address *what* content is likely to appear, not *how difficult* individual questions will be.

---

## 9. Bottom Line

**Teach to the standards, not the eras.** The highest-leverage specific bets for June 2026 are the return of Containment (11.09a.2) and American Nationalism (11.03a.1), a rebound of Constitutional Foundations (11.02), and a Civil Rights anchor for the Civic Literacy Essay. Treat the Prosperity-and-Depression unit as due for a rest rather than a comeback, and give students a brief defensive pass over the never-tested canon — the Cuban Missile Crisis, Pearl Harbor, and Hoover vs. FDR — in case the examiners finally reach for fresh material.
