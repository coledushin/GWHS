# U.S. History & Government Regents Exam
## Question-Trend Analysis and June 2026 Content Forecast

*Prepared May 2026 — Based on nine administrations, June 2023 – January 2026*

---

## Executive Summary

This report analyzes nine consecutive administrations of the New York State Regents Examination in U.S. History and Government (June 2023 through January 2026) to identify how exam content is distributed and how it shifts from test to test. The goal is to forecast the most probable content of the June 2026 examination at both the framework-unit level and the more granular content-standard level.

The central finding is that Regents content is **not random**. It follows identifiable patterns: a stable structural framework, a small set of "iron-man" sub-topics that recur almost every exam, and a rotation logic in which heavily used content rests and dormant content returns. *Predictions built on era-level frequency alone are insufficient — the data show that topics trend in and out, and that recent saturation in one part of the exam (for example, the essays) suppresses the same content elsewhere.*

## 1. Data and Methodology

The analysis draws on a question-by-question itemization of every released Regents exam from June 2023 through January 2026. Each multiple-choice question was coded to its NYS framework unit (11.01–11.11) and, where possible, to its specific leaf-level content standard (e.g., 11.09a.2, Containment Policies). Each question was further tagged for stimulus type (text, visual, map, data, multiple) and for whether it requires outside information ("OI") beyond the document itself.

**Note on the two most recent exams:** the source itemization workbook covers administrations through June 2025. The August 2025 and January 2026 exams were coded directly from the released PDFs for this report. Those two columns are therefore the analyst's readings rather than the workbook author's official coding; they are directionally reliable but should be treated as estimates.

All counts below refer to Part I multiple-choice questions unless the written portions (Part II short-essay questions and the Part III Civic Literacy Essay) are explicitly named.

## 2. How Regents Questions Behave: Six Structural Trends

Before forecasting specific content, it is essential to understand the recurring mechanics that govern how the exam is built. Six patterns emerge consistently across all nine administrations.

### Trend 1 — The format is fixed; the content rotates
Every exam follows the same architecture: 28 stimulus-based multiple-choice questions, two short-essay questions (each built on a pair of documents), and one Civic Literacy document-based essay. Every single question is anchored to a source — there are no "naked" recall questions. This stability means the variable to predict is not format but content placement.

### Trend 2 — Roughly two-thirds of questions require outside knowledge
Across the dataset, about **67%** of multiple-choice questions cannot be answered from the document alone; they require the student to supply outside information. Critically, this burden is unevenly distributed across both content and stimulus type, which has direct teaching implications (see Section 4).

### Trend 3 — Within each unit, questions cluster on a few "iron-man" standards
A framework unit is not tested evenly across its sub-topics. Cold War questions overwhelmingly mean *Containment*; the Depression era overwhelmingly means the *New Deal*. Predicting at the era level alone misses this concentration — the unit label is a container for a handful of repeatedly favored standards (see Section 5).

### Trend 4 — The written and multiple-choice portions are complementary
When a topic anchors an essay, it tends to be rested in the multiple-choice section, and vice versa. This is the single most important refinement to era-level forecasting. The Prosperity-and-Depression unit (11.07) is the clearest example: its multiple-choice presence has collapsed to almost nothing precisely as it has dominated the essays.

### Trend 5 — Topics rotate; rebounds are about twice as common as sustained declines
When a unit's question count drops from one exam to the next, the most likely next move is a rebound. Across all dips in the dataset, the following exam **rebounded 50% of the time, stayed flat 31%, and fell further only 19%**. Equivalently, a low reading is more often a trough than the start of a trend. The corollary also holds: two unusually high exams in a row typically precede a rest.

### Trend 6 — Constitutional Foundations is the backbone
Across nine exams, 11.02 (Constitutional Foundations) averages nearly five questions — the highest of any unit — and has anchored both multiple-choice sets and the short-essay section. It is the most reliable single unit on the exam, which makes its recent two-exam decline (Section 3) the most consequential anomaly in the current data.

## 6. The Written Portions and the "Rest" Effect

The complementarity between the essays and multiple choice (Trend 4) is the strongest argument against naive era-level forecasting. Below is the content of the written portions on the two most recent exams.

| Component | August 2025 | January 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Short-Essay 1 | Declaration + Constitution (11.02) | Great Depression charts + FDR (11.07) |
| Short-Essay 2 | FDR "Forgotten Man" letter (11.07) | Erie Canal / Market Revolution (11.03) |
| Civic Literacy Essay | Government Regulation of Business (11.05) | Prohibition (11.07) |

**11.07 occupied three of the six written slots across these two exams.** The correct inference is *not* that the Depression era is "building toward" a multiple-choice comeback — it is that the exam has been satisfying its 11.07 coverage obligation through the essays, which is precisely why its multiple-choice count has gone to near zero. By the same rotation logic, recently-used essay topics (Prohibition, the Erie Canal, Government Regulation of Business) are unlikely to anchor the June 2026 essays.

### Most probable June 2026 essay anchors
- **Civic Literacy Essay:** Civil Rights Movement (11.10) is the leading candidate — a perennial civic-issue topic not used recently. Women's Suffrage and Immigration are secondary candidates.
- **Short-Essay sets:** fresh material from the Cold War (11.09) and the WWI era (11.06) is most likely, as neither has anchored a recent written prompt.

## 7. June 2026 Forecast and the Reasoning Behind It

The forecast separates two things easily conflated: the **magnitude** of a prediction (a high or low question count) and the **confidence** in it (how likely the actual count lands in the stated range). These are independent. A low forecast can be a high-confidence call — predicting that World War II stays at one or two questions is one of the surest statements in this report. Accordingly, the highest-confidence units below are a deliberate mix of probable rebounds and stable lows.

A second discipline applies throughout: **the 28-question budget.** Because the ranges collectively exceed 28, not every unit can land high. The optimistic rebound calls compete with one another for a finite number of slots, which is a structural reason to weight them toward the lower ends of their ranges.

### High-confidence calls

**11.02 Constitutional Foundations — forecast 4–6, High.** The highest-volume unit on the exam (5.1 average) sat at its normal 6 as recently as August 2025, then dropped sharply to 2 in January 2026 — a single-exam anomaly, not a sustained decline (an earlier coding error had it looking like a two-exam slide). The base-rate rebound logic (Trend 5), the unit's status as the backbone (Trend 6), and its freedom to return to multiple choice now that it last anchored an essay in August 2025 (Trend 4) all point upward. The confidence is in the *direction and presence*; the range stays 4–6 rather than a committed 5–6 only because a one-exam plunge this steep is itself a reminder that the unit can swing.

**11.09 Cold War — forecast 3–5, High.** The unit rebounded to 4 in January 2026 and is a reliable rebounder. The decisive factor is its iron-man standard, Containment (11.09a.2), which has appeared in eight of the nine exams studied (14 questions lifetime, including the Marshall Plan question in January 2026). It is less "due to return" than simply ever-present: at least one containment question is the safest single-standard bet on the exam. The unit is also fresh for the essays, so nothing is suppressing its multiple-choice presence.

**11.08 World War II — forecast 1–2, High.** A structurally light unit, range-bound at one or two questions in five of the last six administrations (the lone exception being a 6-question spike in June 2024). Low and predictable.

**11.01 Colonial Foundations — forecast 2, High.** The single most stable unit on the test: exactly two questions on each of the last four exams. The narrowest forecast in the report, and among the most confident.

### Medium-confidence calls

**11.07 Prosperity & Depression — forecast 1–3, Medium (a tempered contrarian call).** The recent record is genuinely low — `2, 2, 2, 2, 1` over the last five exams — and that stability is the strongest reason to forecast below the unit's 3.0 average. But three caveats keep this from being a high-confidence "stays low" call. First, the "never rebounds" pattern is thin: it reflects one long glide down from the January 2024 spike, not repeated independent failures to recover. Second, the New Deal (11.07c.5) is the second most-tested standard on the entire exam and is still appearing (Jan 2026), so the unit's flagship content is active, not dormant. Third, and most important, the suppression mechanism is double-edged: 11.07 has filled three of the last six essay slots, which by the same two-in-a-row logic makes the *essays* the thing most due to rotate off it. If the June 2026 essays drop the Depression era, the force holding 11.07 out of multiple choice disappears, and it could climb back toward its baseline of 3. The direction (below average) is defensible; certainty about it is not.

**11.06 Rise of American Power / WWI — forecast 3–4, Medium-High.** A reliable high-volume performer that always appears, which makes its *presence* a near-certainty. But the specific count is more constrained than a 4–5 call would imply. After peaking at 8 in January 2025, it has settled to 4, 3, 4 — at or below its 4.6 average. Compounding the downside, its iron-man standard, the Spanish-American War (11.06a.2), appeared in both of the last two exams and is due to rest under the same two-in-a-row logic applied elsewhere. The honest call is 3–4: high confidence it appears, medium confidence in the exact range.

**11.04 Reconstruction — forecast 2–4, Medium-High.** The most document-friendly unit, always present at low volume. The range is wide enough to capture its normal variance, which is why confidence is reasonable; the recent rebound to 4 may settle back toward 2–3.

**11.05 Industrialization — forecast 3–5, Medium.** Genuinely volatile, with a lifetime range from 1 to 8. It anchored the August 2025 Civic Literacy Essay, so a multiple-choice surge is unlikely, but its swings make any specific range only a medium-confidence bet.

**11.10 Civil Rights & Social Change — forecast 2–4, Medium.** Oscillates between 2 and 4 in recent exams. It is the leading candidate to anchor the June 2026 Civic Literacy Essay; if it does, its multiple-choice count may stay capped rather than spike, since the exam tends not to double up on a single unit.

**11.03 Expansion & Sectionalism — forecast 2–5, Medium.** The rebound thesis is sound — the unit is below its 4.4 average and its staple standard, American Nationalism (11.03a.1), is the most overdue on the exam — but it is unproven. The unit has been stuck at 2 for two straight exams, so the wide range reflects real uncertainty about whether the rebound arrives in June or later.

**11.11 U.S. in a Changing World — forecast 1–2, Medium.** Structurally light and erratic: absent (0) on three of the nine exams and never above 2 (an earlier coding error had overstated August 2025 at 3; corrected, it was 1, because the 2000-election questions belong to 11.02d.3). A consistently minor unit.

## 8. Limitations

- Nine administrations is a modest sample; single-exam swings can be large, and confidence levels reflect tendencies, not guarantees.
- The August 2025 and January 2026 codings are the analyst's readings of released PDFs, not the official itemization, and may differ at the margins from the test author's intended coding.
- Rotation logic is probabilistic. The exam writers are not bound by it, and an unusually high or low reading can persist contrary to the base rate — the 11.02 forecast in particular carries real uncertainty.
- Forecasts address *what* content is likely to appear, not *how difficult* individual questions will be.

## 9. Bottom Line: A Research-Based 9-Day Review Plan

**Teach to the standards, not the eras — and judge predictions by their reliability, not their size.** The most reliable expectations for June 2026 are not all "big" calls: alongside the probable rebounds of Constitutional Foundations (11.02) and the Cold War (11.09) — especially the near-certain Containment question (11.09a.2) — sit equally confident *low* calls for World War II and Colonial Foundations. Prosperity & Depression (11.07) is the genuine wildcard: it has sat at 1–2 for five exams, but the essays that have been absorbing its coverage are themselves due to rotate off it, which could send it back toward its baseline of 3 — so treat it as a watch item, not a settled low. Because the forecast ranges collectively exceed the 28-question budget, the rebounds will be partial: not every high-volume unit can recover in the same sitting.

The rest of this section turns the forecast into a concrete review sequence. It assumes a teacher delivering review over **nine 45-minute class periods**, and it is built as much on cognitive-science evidence about *how* people retain material as on the data about *what* to review.

### 9.1 The five principles the plan is built on

1. **Retrieval practice beats rereading.** The act of pulling information *out* of memory — quizzing, brain-dumps, free recall — builds durable memory far better than reviewing notes. In the foundational study, students who practiced recall retained about 80% of material after a week versus 34% for those who reread (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). The largest meta-analysis to date (242 studies, ~169,000 learners) ranked practice testing and distributed practice as the two most effective techniques studied (Donoghue & Hattie, 2021). **Practical rule: every class period should be mostly students retrieving, not the teacher re-presenting.**

2. **Space it, and let students sleep between passes.** The same content revisited across several days outperforms one long session, because each gap allows sleep-dependent consolidation. An expanding cadence — revisit after ~1 day, then ~3, then ~7 — approximates the ideal. This plan therefore re-quizzes earlier days' content at the start of every period rather than reviewing each unit once and moving on.

3. **Block first, then interleave.** Pure mixing of topics from the start can overwhelm students still building basic knowledge; a hybrid — teach a unit in a focused block, then mix it into cumulative practice with other units — produces the most robust retention, especially for lower achievers (Hwang, 2025). Interleaving also mirrors the real exam, where consecutive questions jump across eras and demand that students decide *which* knowledge applies.

4. **Make retrieval higher-order, not just factual.** Quizzing students with analysis-and-application questions (not just "who/when") produces larger gains on the higher-order items that dominate a stimulus-based exam (Agarwal et al., 2021). On this exam that means practicing with actual documents and "why/which-best-supports" prompts, not flashcard facts alone.

5. **Practice in the exam's format.** Roughly two-thirds of multiple-choice questions require outside knowledge, and the share varies sharply by unit (Section 4). Students must rehearse the two distinct moves the test demands: *reasoning from a document* (best trained on document-friendly units like Reconstruction and WWII) and *supplying outside knowledge the document only hints at* (essential for the Cold War, Industrialization, and the Depression era).

A corollary worth stating plainly: **cramming feels effective and isn't.** Massed study reliably produces higher confidence but lower delayed-test performance than spaced retrieval. The plan below trades the comfort of re-reading for the documented payoff of spaced, mixed self-testing.

### 9.2 Data-driven priorities to load into the plan

- **Highest-value content (teach thoroughly):** 11.02 Constitutional Foundations, 11.09 Cold War (with **Containment** as the single safest standard), 11.06 Rise of American Power/WWI, 11.03 Expansion & Sectionalism (with **American Nationalism**, the most overdue standard).
- **Best point-per-minute (lean on the document):** 11.04 Reconstruction and 11.08 World War II are the most document-friendly units — train students to answer these from the stimulus.
- **Must-be-memorized (document won't save you):** 11.07 Depression, 11.09 Cold War, 11.05 Industrialization, 11.02 — all high on outside-information demand.
- **Likely essay anchors:** Civil Rights (11.10) for the Civic Literacy Essay; fresh Cold War (11.09) or WWI-era (11.06) content for the short-essay sets.
- **Defensive 10 minutes:** the never-tested canon — Cuban Missile Crisis, Pearl Harbor, Hoover vs. FDR — in case the examiners reach for fresh material.

### 9.3 The nine-day sequence

Every period follows the same rhythm, which bakes in principles 1–3 automatically:

> **(a) 5-min retrieval warm-up** on *earlier* days' material (spacing + interleaving) → **(b) ~20-min focused block** on the day's unit, taught through documents and higher-order questions → **(c) ~15-min mixed practice** with real past-exam items → **(d) 5-min exit brain-dump:** students write everything they remember from today, then check it.

| Day | Focus block (new content) | Warm-up retrieves | Format skill rehearsed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Diagnostic (10 MC, one per unit) + **11.02 Constitutional Foundations** | — | Reading the question before the document |
| **2** | **11.09 Cold War** + drill **Containment** | Day 1 | Spotting "outside-info" question stems |
| **3** | **11.05 Industrialization** + **11.06 American Power/WWI** | Days 1–2 | Cartoons & visuals (93% need outside knowledge) |
| **4** | **11.03 Expansion** (esp. American Nationalism) + **11.10 Civil Rights** | Days 1–3 (mixed) | Document-based vs. outside-info sorting |
| **5** | **11.07 Depression** (memorize-heavy) + **11.04 Reconstruction** & **11.08 WWII** (document-friendly) | Days 2–4 (mixed) | Answering directly from the stimulus |
| **6** | **11.01 Colonial** + **11.11 Modern era** + defensive blind-spot pass; **first full 28-question interleaved MC test** | Days 1–5 (cumulative) | Full Part I timing & stamina |
| **7** | Targeted reteach of Day-6 weak spots + **Short-Essay (SEQ) workshop** | Days 1–6 (cumulative) | SEQ structure: context → relationship (cause/effect, similarity/difference, turning point) |
| **8** | **Civic Literacy Essay workshop** (use a past Civil-Rights or comparable prompt) | Cumulative | CLE 3-bullet template; analyzing 6 documents fast |
| **9** | **Full timed mock** (or full Part I + one SEQ) → score by unit → overnight targeted list | Cumulative | Whole-exam conditions |

**Why this order.** The highest-value and most memory-intensive units (11.02, 11.09) are front-loaded so they get the most spaced repetitions before exam day — they appear in every subsequent warm-up. Document-friendly units (11.04, 11.08) come later and lighter because they reward a *skill* (reading the source) more than accumulated knowledge. Essays land on Days 7–8, after the content base exists to draw on, and the mock on Day 9 is diagnostic, not instructional — its purpose is to generate each student's final, personalized overnight review list.

**Two cautions for the final 48 hours.** First, resist the urge to introduce new content late; spend Days 8–9 consolidating and rehearsing format, because last-minute cramming inflates confidence without improving delayed performance. Second, protect sleep between Days 8 and 9 and the night before the exam — consolidation is when the spacing actually pays off.
